Configure, then Start. Hover the bar to sample the traction. Lower damping → crisper wave, more bounces; higher → settles to F/A faster.

This example models the iron bar as a 1D elastic rod and solves the wave equation ρ ü = E u′′ so that a suddenly applied force launches a stress wave that travels at the longitudinal wave speed c = √(E/ρ). The bar can be colored by the local axial stress σ = E·ε (toggle Colors): red for tension, blue for compression, white for zero. Coloring is independent of Slow motion — you can watch a fast static deformation colored, or an uncolored slow-motion wave.

Apply a force on the right end (with the left end held fixed) and in Slow motion you can watch the red tension travel from the right end through the bar until the whole bar reaches the uniform equilibrium stress σ = F/A. You can apply forces on both ends at the same time (uncheck “Hold left end fixed” to pull the left end too); balanced outward forces settle to uniform tension, with waves entering from both ends.

Why does it keep bouncing? The bar is an ideal 1D elastic rod solving the lossless wave equation ρü = E u′′. A sudden force launches a stress wave at c = √(E/ρ) that reflects at the ends. In a truly lossless rod these reflections continue forever — that is correct mechanics, not a bug. Real steel has internal (material) damping that bleeds energy over many reflections until the bar rests at the static equilibrium σ = F/A. The Damping control models this: lower it for a crisp front that bounces longer; raise it to settle to F/A faster (at the cost of a softer wave front). Tune the Time scale to slow the animation down further. Configure everything, then press Start to launch the wave.

The first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor at a point is P = diag(σ, 0) (uniaxial; lateral surfaces are stress-free). Hovering samples the local σ and shows the traction t = P·n (green) decomposed into its normal component tn = (t·n) n (blue) and shear component ts = t − tn (orange) on the plane whose normal n you configure. When the cursor leaves the bar, the last sampled point is kept. Rotate the normal and notice how a purely axial stress produces both normal and shear traction on an inclined plane — why stress is a tensor, not a scalar.
